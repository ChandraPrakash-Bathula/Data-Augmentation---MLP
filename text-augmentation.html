03 · Augmentation

Text augmentation

Make new training sentences that mean the same thing and carry the same label but use different words. It's cheap and often helps on small datasets. It's also easy to get wrong, because one changed word can reverse a sentence's meaning.

Why text is harder than images

What

Text augmentation applies small, label-preserving edits to sentences (substituting, inserting, reordering, deleting, or rewriting) to create additional training examples.

How

Edits happen at three levels: characters (typos), words (synonyms, swaps, deletions), and whole sentences (back-translation, paraphrasing with a language model).

Why

Labelled text is expensive. Every review needs a human. Augmentation shows the model that "the film was great" and "the movie was excellent" belong to the same class, so it learns meaning rather than memorising particular words.

The core difficultyImages are continuous: rotate a cat by 5° and you still have a cat. Text is discrete, and there's no "slightly different" version of a word. "good" → "not good" is a one-word edit that flips the label. Every text technique trades diversity (how different the new sentence is) against fidelity (whether the label is still correct).

    The four EDA operations

    EDA (Easy Data Augmentation, Wei & Zou, 2019) packages four word-level operations. A single parameter α controls how many words change: n = max(1, α · sentence_length).

    1 · Synonym Replacement (SR)

    What

    Pick n non-stopwords at random and replace each with a synonym. "The quick fox" → "The fast fox".

    How

    Skip stopwords (the, is, and, not…), because replacing them adds noise without adding meaning. Look up synonyms in WordNet and choose one at random.

    Why

    It teaches lexical invariance: the label depends on meaning, not on which specific word the reviewer happened to choose. This is the safest and most widely used operation.

    import random, nltk
from nltk.corpus import wordnet
nltk.download("wordnet"); nltk.download("omw-1.4")

STOP = {"the","a","an","is","was","and","or","but","i","it","this","to","of","in","not","no","never"}

def synonyms(word):
    out = {l.name().replace("_", " ").lower()
           for s in wordnet.synsets(word) for l in s.lemmas()}
    out.discard(word)
    return list(out)

def synonym_replacement(sentence, n=1):
    words = sentence.split()
    candidates = [i for i, w in enumerate(words) if w.lower() not in STOP and synonyms(w)]
    for i in random.sample(candidates, min(n, len(candidates))):
        words[i] = random.choice(synonyms(words[i]))
    return " ".join(words)

    2 · Random Insertion (RI)

    What

    Take a synonym of a random word and insert it at a random position. "The plot was boring" → "The plot was tedious boring".

    How

    Repeat n times. Because the inserted word is related to the sentence's own vocabulary, it stays on topic.

    Why

    It makes the model robust to extra or redundant words, which real reviews are full of. The result is often ungrammatical, and that's acceptable: bag-of-words and embedding-average models care much more about which words appear than about their order.

    3 · Random Swap (RS)

    What

    Pick two positions and swap the words, n times. This is the technique in Text_Augmentation.ipynb.

    How

    i, j = random.sample(range(len(words)), 2), then words[i], words[j] = words[j], words[i].

    Why and when

    It encourages robustness to word order, which is useful for topic classification. It's risky for sentiment: swapping "not" moves the negation onto a different word, so "not boring, just long" can become "boring, not just long".

    4 · Random Deletion (RD)

    What

    Delete each word independently with probability p = α. Always keep at least one word.

    How

    [w for w in words if random.random() > p] or [random.choice(words)]

    Why

    This is dropout on the input. The model can't rely on any single word, so it has to spread its evidence across the whole sentence. The danger is deleting the one word that carries the label, such as "not".

    def random_swap(sentence, n=1):
    words = sentence.split()
    for _ in range(n):
        if len(words) < 2: break
        i, j = random.sample(range(len(words)), 2)
        words[i], words[j] = words[j], words[i]
    return " ".join(words)

def random_deletion(sentence, p=0.1):
    words = sentence.split()
    kept = [w for w in words if random.random() > p]
    return " ".join(kept or [random.choice(words)])

def random_insertion(sentence, n=1):
    words = sentence.split()
    for _ in range(n):
        pool = [w for w in words if synonyms(w)]
        if not pool: break
        words.insert(random.randint(0, len(words)), random.choice(synonyms(random.choice(pool))))
    return " ".join(words)

def eda(sentence, alpha=0.1, n_aug=4):
    n = max(1, int(alpha * len(sentence.split())))
    ops = [lambda s: synonym_replacement(s, n), lambda s: random_insertion(s, n),
           lambda s: random_swap(s, n),         lambda s: random_deletion(s, alpha)]
    return [random.choice(ops)(sentence) for _ in range(n_aug)]
    What the EDA paper foundGains were largest on small training sets: about +3% average accuracy with 500 examples, and under +1% on full datasets. Training with EDA on 50% of the data matched training without EDA on 100%. Suggested settings: α ≈ 0.05–0.1, with more variants per sentence when data is scarce (16 for ~500 examples, 4 for 5,000+).

    Character-level noise (typos)

    What

    Substitute, swap, delete or insert individual characters: "quick" → "qiuck", "quock", "quik".

    How

    A realistic version substitutes a keyboard neighbour (e → w, r, d) instead of a random letter. The notebook's version picks any random letter, including over spaces and punctuation.

    Why

    Real user text, such as tweets, chat, OCR output and search queries, is noisy. A model trained only on clean text sees "terible" as an unknown token and loses that evidence. Typo augmentation teaches it to cope.

    NEIGHBOURS = {"a":"qwsz","e":"wrds","i":"ujko","o":"iklp","r":"edft","t":"rfgy","s":"awedxz","n":"bhjm"}

def keyboard_typo(sentence, n=1):
    chars = list(sentence)
    idx = [i for i, c in enumerate(chars) if c.lower() in NEIGHBOURS]
    for i in random.sample(idx, min(n, len(idx))):
        chars[i] = random.choice(NEIGHBOURS[chars[i].lower()])
    return "".join(chars)

    Sentence-level: back-translation and paraphrasing

    Back-translation

    What

    Translate to a pivot language and back: EN → FR → EN. "The quick brown fox jumps" → "Le renard brun rapide saute" → "The fast brown fox leaps".

    How

    Use a pretrained translation model. The notebook mocks this step. Below is a real version using MarianMT from Hugging Face. Sampling during decoding (do_sample=True) gives more varied paraphrases.

    Why

    Translation models are trained to preserve meaning, so the round trip gives fluent, grammatical paraphrases with new wording and new structure, which word-level EDA can't produce. It was popularised for machine translation by Sennrich et al. (2016) and used for text classification in UDA (Xie et al., 2020).

    from transformers import MarianMTModel, MarianTokenizer

def load(name):
    return MarianTokenizer.from_pretrained(name), MarianMTModel.from_pretrained(name)

en_fr = load("Helsinki-NLP/opus-mt-en-fr")
fr_en = load("Helsinki-NLP/opus-mt-fr-en")

def translate(texts, tok_model, sample=False):
    tok, model = tok_model
    batch = tok(texts, return_tensors="pt", padding=True, truncation=True)
    out = model.generate(**batch, do_sample=sample, top_k=50, max_new_tokens=128)
    return tok.batch_decode(out, skip_special_tokens=True)

def back_translate(texts):
    return translate(translate(texts, en_fr), fr_en, sample=True)

back_translate(["The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog."])

    Paraphrasing with a language model

    Prompt a seq2seq model (T5, BART) or an LLM: "Rewrite this review in different words. Keep the sentiment unchanged." This gives the most diverse and fluent output, at the highest compute cost. Because the model is generating text, check a sample of outputs: it can drift in sentiment, add facts, or tone down strong opinions.

    Embedding-based and contextual replacement

    What

    Replace a word with its nearest neighbour in an embedding space (word2vec / GloVe), or let a masked language model (BERT) fill in a masked word using the surrounding context.

    How

    Similarity is usually cosine similarity: cos(a,b) = a·b / (‖a‖‖b‖). In the notebook's toy vectors, quick=[0.10,0.20] and fast=[0.11,0.19] have cosine ≈ 0.998, so they're near-synonyms.

    Why, and the catch

    Embeddings cover slang and domain words that WordNet doesn't. But they encode "used in similar contexts", not "means the same". In word2vec, "bad" is one of the closest neighbours of "good", because both appear in sentences like "the movie was ___". For sentiment tasks, that substitution flips the label.

    import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F
quick, fast = torch.tensor([0.10, 0.20]), torch.tensor([0.11, 0.19])
F.cosine_similarity(quick, fast, dim=0)   # tensor(0.9981)

    Random deletionLowMediumVery lowLong texts with redundant words
    Character typosLowHighVery lowNoisy user text, search, OCR

    Back-translationHighHighMedium (GPU)Sentiment, intent, any sentence classification
    LLM paraphrasingVery highMedium–highHighFew-shot / very small datasets

    When augmentation breaks the label

    Watch for these
    • Negation: deleting or moving not / never / no / n't reverses sentiment.
    • Antonym-ish neighbours: embedding replacement can turn good → bad or love → hate.
    • Polysemy: WordNet's synonym of "light" (weight) might be "illumination" (lamp), producing nonsense.
    • Token-level tasks: for NER or QA, insertion and deletion shift positions, so the span labels must be updated as well.
    • Domain terms: in medical or legal text, "synonyms" are often not interchangeable.

    Practical defences: exclude negation words and protected terms from edits, keep α small, and read 20–30 augmented samples by hand before training. If you have a reasonable model already, you can also discard augmented samples whose predicted label differs from the original.
